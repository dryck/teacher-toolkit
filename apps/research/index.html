🔊 Noise Monitor

Pedagogical Foundation: Self-Regulated Learning (SRL)

The Noise Monitor is grounded in Self-Regulated Learning theory, developed by educational psychologist Barry J. Zimmerman. SRL describes the process by which learners monitor, evaluate, and adjust their own behaviour in response to real-time feedback. A key component of this cycle is self-monitoring — the ability to observe one's own performance against a goal.

By making collective noise levels visible on a shared screen, the Noise Monitor externalises an otherwise invisible signal. Students gain immediate, objective feedback and learn to regulate group behaviour without direct teacher intervention. This shifts agency from the teacher to the learners — a shift consistently linked to improved motivation and deeper learning.

"Self-regulated learners are distinguished by their proactive efforts to seek out information and to take the steps necessary to master it."
— Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview. Theory Into Practice, 41(2), 64–70.

Key research

  • Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview. Theory Into Practice, 41(2), 64–70.
  • Zimmerman, B. J., Bonner, S., & Kovach, R. (1996). Developing self-regulated learners: Beyond achievement to self-efficacy. American Psychological Association.
  • Dignath, C., & Büttner, G. (2008). Components of fostering self-regulated learning among students. Metacognition and Learning, 3, 231–264.

📊 Quick Poll

Pedagogical Foundation: Formative Assessment & Hinge Questions

The Quick Poll implements formative assessment — assessment used not to grade students, but to inform and adjust teaching in real time. This approach was established by Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam in their landmark 1998 review of over 250 studies, which found that formative assessment produces learning gains with effect sizes between 0.4 and 0.7 — among the highest of any educational intervention.

A Quick Poll functions as a hinge question: a diagnostic check placed at a pivot point in the lesson. If most students understand, the teacher moves on. If not, instruction is adjusted immediately — before misconceptions solidify. Students benefit from anonymous participation, which lowers the emotional stakes of being "wrong" and encourages honest responses.

"The goal is to gather diagnostic information fast enough to change instruction in real time, within the same lesson period."
— Wiliam, D. (2011). Embedded Formative Assessment. Solution Tree Press.

Key research

  • Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Inside the Black Box: Raising Standards Through Classroom Assessment. Phi Delta Kappan, 80(2), 139–148.
  • Leahy, S., Lyon, C., Thompson, M., & Wiliam, D. (2005). Classroom assessment: Minute by minute, day by day. Educational Leadership, 63(3), 18–24.
  • Black, P., Harrison, C., Lee, C., Marshall, B., & Wiliam, D. (2004). Working inside the black box. Phi Delta Kappan, 86(1), 9–22.

🎲 Random Picker

Pedagogical Foundation: Equity, Engagement & Accountable Talk

The Random Picker addresses one of the most persistent inequities in classroom participation: when teachers call on volunteers, the same students answer repeatedly, while others disengage and become invisible. Research consistently shows that hand-raising skews participation toward high-achieving students and disadvantages female students and introverts.

Random selection creates accountable talk: every student knows they may be called on at any moment, which increases preparation, focus, and engagement across the whole class. A 2022 study found a statistically significant increase in self-reported class participation when randomised cold-calling was implemented. Crucially, randomisation also signals to students that the teacher's attention is unbiased and fair.

"Random student selection is the process of randomly selecting students to participate. Now, at any time, any student may be selected. All students must remain engaged and cannot opt out."
— Keen, C. (2006). University Study Finds Random Student Selection Keeps Students Engaged. Kagan Online Magazine.

Key research

  • Dallimore, E. J., Hertenstein, J. H., & Platt, M. B. (2010). Class participation in accounting courses. Issues in Accounting Education, 25, 613–629.
  • Eddy, S. L., Brownell, S. E., & Wenderoth, M. P. (2014). Gender gaps in achievement and participation in multiple introductory biology classrooms. CBE Life Sciences Education, 13, 478–492.
  • Cold calls to enhance class participation and student engagement (2022). Singapore Management University. ink.library.smu.edu.sg
  • Kagan, S. (1994). Cooperative Learning. Kagan Publishing.

⏱️ Visual Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Executive Function & Time Perception

The Visual Timer addresses a fundamental cognitive challenge: time blindness. For children — especially those with ADHD, autism, or executive function difficulties — time is an invisible abstraction. A standard clock requires active arithmetic ("it's 10:47, the lesson ends at 11:05, so…"), which consumes working memory and takes focus away from the task.

A visual timer externalises time: it converts an abstract concept into a concrete, shrinking visual cue. This is grounded in research on executive function, the set of cognitive skills that govern planning, task initiation, and self-regulation. When time is made visible, students can self-pace their work, reduce transition anxiety, and build an internal sense of time over repeated exposure.

A 2024 study by Hallez & Rebecchi demonstrated that the presence of a visual timer during a maths assessment reduced anxiety and improved performance. Wider research confirms benefits for on-task behaviour, smoother transitions, and reduced reliance on teacher prompting.

"Tools that make time concrete can facilitate children's time perception development."
— Neufeld & Stewart (2023), cited in Visual Timers and Classroom Assessment Anxiety, Preprints.org (2025).

Key research

  • Hallez, Q., & Rebecchi, C. (2024). Visual timers reduce assessment anxiety and improve math performance. Frontiers in Education.
  • Demetriou, E. A. et al. (2018). Autism spectrum disorders: a meta-analysis of executive function. Molecular Psychiatry, 23(5), 1198–1204.
  • Time Timer research summary: timetimer.com/blogs/news
  • Preprints.org (2025). Visual Timers and On-Task Behaviour in Classroom Assessments. preprints.org

🎯 Zones Check-In

Pedagogical Foundation: Zones of Regulation & Social-Emotional Learning (SEL)

Zones Check-In is built on the Zones of Regulation framework, developed by occupational therapist Leah Kuypers (2011), in which children learn to recognize and name their emotional state using four colour zones. It sits within the broader Social-Emotional Learning movement formalized by CASEL (the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning), which treats self-awareness and self-management as core, teachable competencies — not soft extras.

By having students check in anonymously at the start of a lesson, the teacher sees the class's emotional climate before teaching begins, and can adapt pacing or approach accordingly. For the student, the simple act of naming a feeling — "I'm in the yellow zone" rather than a vague sense of unease — builds the emotional vocabulary that Zones of Regulation identifies as the first step toward self-regulation. Full anonymity and no per-student history are deliberate: the goal is a classroom-level signal, not a behavioural record on any individual child.

"The Zones of Regulation curriculum is designed to help students gain skills in consciously regulating their actions, which in turn can lead to increased control and problem solving abilities."
— Kuypers, L. (2011). The Zones of Regulation: A Curriculum Designed to Foster Self-Regulation and Emotional Control. Think Social Publishing.

Key research

  • Kuypers, L. (2011). The Zones of Regulation: A Curriculum Designed to Foster Self-Regulation and Emotional Control. Think Social Publishing.
  • CASEL (Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning). CASEL's SEL Framework — the "5 Competencies." casel.org
  • Durlak, J. A., Weissberg, R. P., Dymnicki, A. B., Taylor, R. D., & Schellinger, K. B. (2011). The impact of enhancing students' social and emotional learning: A meta-analysis of school-based universal interventions. Child Development, 82(1), 405–432.

🎫 Exit Ticket

Pedagogical Foundation: Checking for Understanding (Fisher & Frey, 2007)

Exit Ticket implements formative assessment done at the very end of a lesson, based on Fisher & Frey's "Checking for Understanding" model. Its three questions are pitched at three distinct levels: knowledge (what was learned), understanding (how confident the student feels), and metacognition (what's still unclear).

The point is for the teacher to learn what actually landed before the next lesson starts, not after a test makes it too late to act. Because it takes under a minute and carries no grade, it surfaces honest confusion that a formal assessment would otherwise hide until it's too costly to fix.

Key research

  • Fisher, D., & Frey, N. (2007). Checking for Understanding: Formative Assessment Techniques for Your Classroom. ASCD.
  • Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Inside the Black Box: Raising Standards Through Classroom Assessment. Phi Delta Kappan, 80(2), 139–148.
  • Angelo, T. A., & Cross, K. P. (1993). Classroom Assessment Techniques: A Handbook for College Teachers. Jossey-Bass.

🎲 Group Generator

Pedagogical Foundation: Kagan Cooperative Learning — Random Grouping (Kagan, 1994)

Group Generator applies Spencer Kagan's cooperative-learning principle that random grouping breaks down social hierarchies, exposes students to classmates they wouldn't normally choose, and prevents the same cliques from re-forming lesson after lesson.

A fast, visibly fair shuffle also removes the social cost of picking teams — no one is left standing last, and no group looks hand-picked. Optional pinning and a "don't repeat recent pairs" option let a teacher balance genuine randomness against real classroom constraints without undermining the fairness the tool exists to provide.

Key research

  • Kagan, S. (1994). Cooperative Learning. Kagan Publishing.
  • Johnson, D. W., & Johnson, R. T. (1999). Learning Together and Alone: Cooperative, Competitive, and Individualistic Learning. Allyn & Bacon.
  • Slavin, R. E. (1995). Cooperative Learning: Theory, Research, and Practice. Allyn & Bacon.

📋 Behaviour Tracker

Pedagogical Foundation: Positive Behaviour Support / PBIS (Sugai & Horner, 2002)

Behaviour Tracker is grounded in Positive Behaviour Support (PBS/PBIS), which holds that behavioural feedback works best when it's individual rather than aimed at the whole class, and when the system of warnings and consequences is transparent and predictable to the student, not arbitrary.

A quiet tap-to-warn interface lets the teacher give that individual feedback without stopping instruction or calling a student out in front of peers, while every student always knows exactly how many warnings they have and what happens at the threshold — the predictability PBS identifies as central to actually changing behaviour, rather than just interrupting it for a moment.

Key research

  • Sugai, G., & Horner, R. H. (2002). The evolution of discipline practices: School-wide positive behavior supports. Child & Family Behavior Therapy, 24(1-2), 23–50.
  • Horner, R. H., Sugai, G., & Anderson, C. M. (2010). Examining the evidence base for school-wide positive behavior support. Focus on Exceptional Children, 42(8), 1–14.
  • Simonsen, B., Fairbanks, S., Briesch, A., Myers, D., & Sugai, G. (2008). Evidence-based practices in classroom management. Education and Treatment of Children, 31(3), 351–380.

🔵 Restorative Circle

Pedagogical Foundation: Restorative Justice in Education (Zehr, 2002; Costello, Wachtel & Wachtel, 2009)

Restorative Circle structures dialogue the way restorative-justice practice does: every voice in the circle is treated as equally worth hearing, no one is interrupted, and any resolution is built together rather than handed down by the teacher.

Because the app supplies the discussion questions for each circle type in advance, the teacher never has to improvise prompts in a tense moment, and a visible "whose turn" indicator removes the disputes over interruption that can derail an unstructured conversation.

Key research

  • Zehr, H. (2002). The Little Book of Restorative Justice. Good Books.
  • Costello, B., Wachtel, J., & Wachtel, T. (2009). The Restorative Practices Handbook for Teachers, Disciplinarians and Administrators. International Institute for Restorative Practices.
  • Gonzalez, T. (2012). Keeping kids in schools: Restorative justice, punitive discipline, and the school-to-prison pipeline. Journal of Law and Education, 41, 281.

🌱 Growth Mindset

Pedagogical Foundation: Growth Mindset (Carol Dweck, 2006)

Growth Mindset Reframer is built directly on Carol Dweck's distinction between a fixed mindset ("I can't") and a growth mindset ("I can't yet") — the finding that the language a child uses about their own ability shapes how they respond to effort and failure.

By turning a self-defeating statement into a "yet" statement in real time, the tool models the reframe rather than just telling a student to think positive. The shared, teacher-moderated Wall of Yet extends this from an individual exercise into a visible, cumulative class norm.

Key research

  • Dweck, C. S. (2006). Mindset: The New Psychology of Success. Random House.
  • Yeager, D. S., & Dweck, C. S. (2012). Mindsets that promote resilience: When students believe that personal characteristics can be developed. Educational Psychologist, 47(4), 302–314.
  • Blackwell, L. S., Trzesniewski, K. H., & Dweck, C. S. (2007). Implicit theories of intelligence predict achievement across an adolescent transition. Child Development, 78(1), 246–263.

🔄 Kagan Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Kagan Cooperative Learning Structures (Spencer Kagan, 1994)

Kagan Timer runs the class through Kagan's named cooperative-learning structures — Think-Pair-Share, Rally Robin, Timed Pair Share, and others — each of which assigns specific roles and time limits so participation isn't left to whoever raises a hand first.

Kagan's own research is the reason this matters: structured interaction raises the share of students actively participating in a discussion from roughly 3% (one student answering at a time) to over 50%, simply by changing the structure of the interaction rather than the content being taught.

Key research

  • Kagan, S. (1994). Cooperative Learning. Kagan Publishing.
  • Kagan, S., & Kagan, M. (2009). Kagan Cooperative Learning. Kagan Publishing.
  • Slavin, R. E. (1995). Cooperative Learning: Theory, Research, and Practice. Allyn & Bacon.

🧩 Choice Board

Pedagogical Foundation: Universal Design for Learning & Self-Determination Theory (CAST, 2018; Deci & Ryan, 1985)

Choice Board applies Universal Design for Learning's principle of multiple means of engagement together with Self-Determination Theory's finding that autonomy — having a real say in how you learn — increases intrinsic motivation, not just compliance.

A 3×3 grid of varied task types (write, draw, explain, build, research…) lets students choose a path suited to how they learn best, while the teacher still sees, in real time, who's engaging and which tasks the class gravitates toward or avoids.

Key research

  • CAST (2018). Universal Design for Learning Guidelines, version 2.2. cast.org
  • Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior. Plenum.
  • Tomlinson, C. A. (2001). How to Differentiate Instruction in Mixed-Ability Classrooms. ASCD.

🎤 Presentation Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Executive Function & Performance Anxiety (Hallez & Rebecchi, 2024)

Presentation Timer extends the same visual-timer research already behind Visual Timer: a shrinking, visible countdown reduces anxiety before and during a timed performance, because it replaces mental arithmetic about how much time is left with something the presenter can simply glance at.

Structured time also teaches the planning side of executive function — a team that has practiced against a real clock learns to budget what they say, not just what they know — while automatic Q&A transitions mean the teacher can watch the room instead of watching a stopwatch.

Key research

  • Hallez, Q., & Rebecchi, C. (2024). Visual timers reduce assessment anxiety and improve math performance. Frontiers in Education.
  • Bodie, G. D. (2010). A racing heart, rattling knees: Physiological, cognitive, and behavioral manifestations of communication apprehension. Communication Quarterly, 58(1), 70–105.

✅ Assessment Checklist

Pedagogical Foundation: Rubric-Based Assessment (Brookhart, 2013)

Project Assessment Checklist is built on rubric-based assessment as described by Susan Brookhart: when criteria are explicit and known in advance, the quality of student work improves, and a teacher's own scoring becomes more consistent because it's anchored to criteria rather than a holistic gut impression.

Walking the room and tapping a level per criterion, rather than assigning one overall grade from memory afterward, also makes real-time feedback and notes possible while the work — and the teamwork — is still happening in front of the teacher.

Key research

  • Brookhart, S. M. (2013). How to Create and Use Rubrics for Formative Assessment and Grading. ASCD.
  • Jonsson, A., & Svingby, G. (2007). The use of scoring rubrics: Reliability, validity and educational consequences. Educational Research Review, 2(2), 130–144.
  • Andrade, H. G. (2005). Teaching with rubrics: The good, the bad, and the ugly. College Teaching, 53(1), 27–31.

❄️ Snowball Wall

Pedagogical Foundation: Snowballing / 1-2-4-All Cooperative Learning

Snowball Wall is built on "snowballing" (also called pyramiding or 1-2-4-all), a cooperative-learning structure in the same family as Kagan structures and widely used in differentiation practice. Students write an idea alone first, then combine it with a partner, then a group of four, before it joins the whole-class wall — each stage builds on the last.

Writing alone first matters most: it ensures every student, including quieter or lower-confidence ones, commits to an idea before any peer influence, guarding against one voice dominating a pair or group. Each merge afterward forces students to justify, compare, and negotiate their idea against a partner's or group's — that negotiation is where most of the cognitive and language work happens, and requiring one shared sticky per group (not everyone keeping their own) forces real synthesis rather than simple concatenation.

Snowballing is better documented as widely-used classroom practice and teacher-training material than as a single named technique with controlled-trial evidence behind it — it draws its credibility from the same cooperative-learning research base as Kagan structures generally, rather than from a study of "snowballing" specifically.

Key research

  • Kagan, S., & Kagan, M. (2009). Kagan Cooperative Learning. Kagan Publishing.
  • Johnson, D. W., & Johnson, R. T. (1999). Making cooperative learning work. Theory Into Practice, 38(2), 67–73.

🌸 Bloom's Question Builder

Pedagogical Foundation: Bloom's Taxonomy + Cold-Calling Techniques

Bloom's Question Builder combines two complementary ideas: Bloom's Taxonomy, a well-established framework for scaffolding the cognitive demand of a question from Remember through Create, and the Individual / Sequential / Persistent cold-calling techniques popularized by Doug Lemov's Teach Like a Champion. A teacher types in a lesson topic and gets a ready-to-ask question bank organized by taxonomy level, alongside a reminder of how to actually call on students to answer.

Deliberately picking a Bloom's level lets a teacher match a question's difficulty to their goal — a quick "Remember" check versus a deeper "Evaluate" discussion — instead of defaulting to whatever question comes to mind in the moment. Pairing that with a chosen questioning technique (cold-calling one student, chaining a second student onto the first's answer, or persistently returning to a student who says "I don't know" instead of letting them opt out) is associated with more equitable participation than hands-up volunteering alone, where the same few students tend to answer every time.

Key research

  • Bloom, B. S. (Ed.). (1956). Taxonomy of Educational Objectives: The Classification of Educational Goals. Longmans, Green.
  • Anderson, L. W., & Krathwohl, D. R. (Eds.). (2001). A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing. Longman.
  • Lemov, D. (2015). Teach Like a Champion 2.0. Jossey-Bass.

🃏 Challenge Deck

Pedagogical Foundation: Differentiation Through Student Choice

Challenge Deck operationalizes differentiation-by-choice, in the same family as Choice Board: rather than the teacher assigning a task by ability tier, students self-select which category — and by extension, which difficulty and pacing — suits them, then draw a random card from it once finished with core work.

Carol Ann Tomlinson's differentiation framework links this kind of student agency to higher engagement and ownership of learning than a single fixed assignment for the whole class. The random-draw-within-a-category mechanic adds low-stakes variety and repeatability: students can keep drawing within their chosen challenge level rather than exhausting a fixed grid, which supports sustained independent work after the main lesson ends.

Key research

  • Tomlinson, C. A. (2014). The Differentiated Classroom: Responding to the Needs of All Learners (2nd ed.). ASCD.
  • Orr, R. Differentiation — practitioner guide on choice-based task design.

🧠 Think Time

Pedagogical Foundation: Wait-Time Research (Rowe, 1974)

Think Time is built directly on Mary Budd Rowe's wait-time research: simply extending the silent pause between asking a question and calling on a student — from the typical under-one-second gap to three seconds or more — measurably increases the length and quality of student answers, the number of students who volunteer, and their confidence in responding.

Most teachers under-estimate how little silence they actually leave; a shared, visible countdown removes the guesswork and makes the pause a deliberate part of classroom routine rather than something that depends on the teacher consciously holding back. Keeping the countdown itself silent (no ticking) is intentional — the goal is a calm thinking space, not a pressure timer.

Key research

  • Rowe, M. B. (1974). Wait-time and rewards as instructional variables. Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 11(2), 81–94.
  • Tobin, K. (1987). The role of wait time in higher cognitive level learning. Review of Educational Research, 57(1), 69–95.

🔁 Rotation Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Station Rotation / Differentiated Instruction (Tomlinson, 2001)

Rotation Timer supports gallery-walk and carousel activities, where groups move between fixed stations to engage with different tasks or texts in turn. Cooperative, station-based structures let a teacher differentiate content and format simultaneously — each station can target a different skill or modality — while keeping the whole class moving through the same shared rhythm.

A single, unambiguous, loud shared signal for "move now" matters more than it seems: without one, rotations drift, some groups linger while others rush, and the teacher spends the activity policing time instead of circulating and observing. A big shared countdown with a clear warning state and chime hands that job to the tool instead.

Key research

  • Tomlinson, C. A. (2001). How to Differentiate Instruction in Mixed-Ability Classrooms. ASCD.
  • Johnson, D. W., & Johnson, R. T. (1999). Making cooperative learning work. Theory Into Practice, 38(2), 67–73.

🌶️ Chili Challenge

Pedagogical Foundation: Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985)

Chili Challenge lets students choose their own task difficulty — 🌶 / 🌶🌶 / 🌶🌶🌶 — instead of the teacher assigning a tier by perceived ability. Deci and Ryan's self-determination theory identifies autonomy as one of three basic psychological needs (alongside competence and relatedness) that drive intrinsic motivation; letting students pick their own challenge level, rather than being sorted into one, protects that sense of autonomy even when the underlying task content is differentiated.

The deliberate choice to label levels with chilies instead of words like "easy/medium/hard" removes the ability-grouping stigma that can otherwise attach to a visible difficulty tier — a student choosing 🌶🌶🌶 is choosing spice, not publicly declaring themselves "the smart one" or avoiding being seen as "behind." The teacher's live dashboard shows the class's chosen spread without exposing who picked what, so a student can also recalibrate mid-task without a public record of having "dropped down."

Key research

  • Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior. Plenum.
  • Tomlinson, C. A. (2014). The Differentiated Classroom: Responding to the Needs of All Learners (2nd ed.). ASCD.

🪜 Help Ladder

Pedagogical Foundation: Scaffolding & the Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978)

Help Ladder makes Vygotsky's scaffolding idea concrete and visible: before a student reaches for the teacher, they work through a sequence of lower-cost resources — their own effort, the board, a classmate, their notes — each of which can resolve the difficulty without teacher intervention. A persistent, displayed hierarchy turns "try something first" from a vague expectation into a specific, checkable routine, which is what actually changes student behaviour rather than just teacher intention.

The second half of the tool, "Ask 3 Before Me," complements the display with a live but fully anonymous signal: a student who has genuinely exhausted the ladder can flag that they still need help without raising a hand or interrupting, and the teacher gets a real-time, no-names count of who's stuck instead of only learning about it when a queue has already formed at their desk. Keeping the signal anonymous matters — the goal is reducing interruption load on the teacher, not creating a visible ranking of who needed help.

Key research

  • Vygotsky, L. S. (1978). Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes. Harvard University Press.
  • Wood, D., Bruner, J. S., & Ross, G. (1976). The role of tutoring in problem solving. Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 17(2), 89–100.

🪙 Coin Flip

Pedagogical Foundation: Divergent Thinking & Creative Constraint (Guilford, 1967)

Coin Flip introduces genuine randomness into an otherwise open-ended creative task — a student choosing freely between "villain's perspective" or "hero's perspective" often burns time on the choice itself, or defaults to whatever feels safest. Handing that choice to a coin removes the decision paralysis entirely and lets the student's energy go straight into the task, a small but real application of J.P. Guilford's foundational work on divergent thinking and fluency as trainable creative skills.

The "Creative Prompt Flip" mode pushes this further: combining several independently-randomized contrasting pairs (a theme, a perspective, a style) generates combinations a student would rarely choose for themselves, deliberately forcing unfamiliar territory. Constraint-driven prompts like this are a well-established creative-writing technique precisely because removing infinite choice tends to produce more original work, not less.

Key research

  • Guilford, J. P. (1967). The Nature of Human Intelligence. McGraw-Hill.
  • Ward, T. B. (2001). Creative cognition, conceptual combination, and the creative writing process. American Psychologist, 56(4), 350–354.

🧘 Break Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Sensory Regulation & Scheduled Movement (Barkley, 1997)

Break Timer supports scheduled sensory and movement breaks, grounded in Russell Barkley's research on self-regulation and ADHD: short, frequent, predictable breaks measurably improve on-task behaviour more reliably than occasional long ones, because they prevent the build-up of dysregulation rather than only responding to it after the fact. A visible menu of specific, low-stakes activities (deep breaths, a short walk, a squeeze toy) gives students a concrete regulation strategy in the moment, rather than an abstract instruction to "calm down."

The "Scheduled Breaks" mode operationalizes the preventive half of this research directly — a fixed-interval reminder for a brief stretch/breathe pause, independent of whether any individual student currently seems dysregulated, since the point is to keep the whole class ahead of the curve rather than intervening only once a student is visibly struggling. Deliberately calm, non-clinical framing (soft colours, no urgent styling) matters here: the tool models the same regulated tone it's trying to teach.

Key research

  • Barkley, R. A. (1997). Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions: Constructing a unifying theory of ADHD. Psychological Bulletin, 121(1), 65–94.
  • Ma, J. K., Le Mare, L., & Gurd, B. J. (2014). Classroom-based high-intensity interval activity improves off-task behaviour in children with ADHD. Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism, 39(12), 1332–1337.

🏠 Homework Menu

Pedagogical Foundation: Autonomy-Supportive Learning (Deci & Ryan, 1985)

Homework Menu applies the same self-determination-theory logic behind Chili Challenge to work done outside the classroom: instead of one assignment handed to everyone, students choose from several options that all target the same learning goal through a different format. The format changes, not the difficulty or the goal — a student who chooses to record a short video explanation instead of writing an essay is still practising the same understanding, just through a channel that suits them better.

Keeping this entirely link-based (no login, no account, no data sent anywhere) matters for a homework tool specifically: it removes every technical barrier between "the teacher shared this" and "the student can actually open it," and a printable version means a lack of a device at home is never a reason a student can't see tonight's options.

Key research

  • Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior. Plenum.
  • Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: A meta-analysis of research findings. Psychological Bulletin, 134(2), 270–300.

💭 Behaviour Reflection

Pedagogical Foundation: Restorative Practice & Collaborative Problem Solving (Ross Greene)

Behaviour Reflection structures the conversation that happens after an incident around understanding and repair rather than blame — closer to Ross Greene's Collaborative Problem Solving approach than a traditional discipline script. Walking through what happened, how the student feels, who was affected, and what they can do about it (in that order) mirrors the restorative-justice principle that repair only becomes possible once a person has actually processed what happened, not before.

Keeping the record private, calm, and entirely local (no account, no cloud, nothing leaves the device) is a deliberate trust signal, not just a technical choice — a reflection tool that felt like it was "reporting" a student to some system would undermine the honesty it's trying to encourage. The searchable history exists for the teacher's own pattern-spotting across time, not for anyone else's use.

Key research

  • Greene, R. W. (2014). Lost at School: Why Our Kids with Behavioral Challenges are Falling Through the Cracks and How We Can Help Them (rev. ed.). Scribner.
  • Zehr, H. (2002). The Little Book of Restorative Justice. Good Books.

🧠 Brainstorm Timer

Pedagogical Foundation: Divergent Thinking & Fluency (Guilford, 1967)

Brainstorm Timer is built around J.P. Guilford's concept of fluency — the sheer number of ideas a person can generate — as a trainable creative skill distinct from judging any single idea's quality. A random everyday object sidesteps the "blank page" problem entirely: there's no wrong place to start, and the constraint of a single familiar object paradoxically makes idea generation easier, not harder, because it gives divergent thinking something concrete to push against.

The live, visible idea count matters as much as the timer itself: watching the number climb in real time turns brainstorming into a shared, energizing activity the whole class experiences together, rather than a private, potentially anxiety-inducing test of individual creativity. Keeping submissions anonymous removes the fear of a "bad" idea being attributed to any one student — which is precisely the condition under which fluency research shows people generate more, not fewer, ideas.

🔍 Behaviour Log

Pedagogical Foundation: ABC Functional Behaviour Analysis (Francine Brower)

Behaviour Log applies Antecedent-Behaviour-Consequence analysis, a core tool in supporting autistic students and others with additional needs: a single incident rarely reveals its own cause, but a pattern across many incidents often does. Logging the activity, possible trigger, and time of day alongside each incident — quickly enough to actually happen in the moment — turns a string of seemingly unrelated "bad days" into visible data a teacher can act on.

The distinction this tool draws from the existing Behaviour Tracker matters: warnings-and-consequences is a classroom-management tool for the whole class in real time, while this is a diagnostic tool for one student's patterns over weeks. Separating "was noise the trigger, or was it the transition itself?" from "how many warnings has Sam had today?" is the difference between managing a moment and actually understanding it.
